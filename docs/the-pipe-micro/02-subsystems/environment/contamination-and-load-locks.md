# Contamination and transfer chambers

A small particle or residue patch can be a significant part of a microcontact. The chamber helps by making exposure controllable. It does not remove material already present on printed parts or generated by moving mechanisms.

## Sources inside and outside

External sources include airborne particles, skin oils and contamination on incoming cassettes. Internal sources include uncured resin, developer residue, wear at tendon guides or carriage contacts, fragments from damaged microtextures and liquid left by wet tools. These sources change friction, wetting, adhesion and optical appearance together.

## The transfer-chamber concept

A small entry compartment accepts a part cassette or a tool. Its outer access closes before it opens into the working region. The compartment can be inspected and brought toward compatible conditions without opening the main chamber. “Load-lock” here does not imply the whole machine must operate under vacuum.

The object should remain mechanically retained during transfer and conditioning. Low weight is not an invitation to rely on a tray's orientation. Printed pockets, lids or simple retainers can prevent uncontrolled motion during gas flow or handling.

## Cleanable geometry

Open drainage paths can make a texture easier to clean and inspect. Blind cavities, dense fibrils and narrow channels can trap resin or debris. A surface that gives good first-cycle release may degrade rapidly if its gaps bridge with contamination.

Replaceable contact tips and sacrificial tool cartridges are therefore interesting even if the arm itself is monolithic. A parked dirty tool should not contaminate every shared fixture.

## Wet and dry work

Capillary tools intentionally introduce a liquid and thus a different surface history. A separate station, cassette or tool set can make that history understandable. This is a possible spatial arrangement, not a requirement to build multiple chambers initially.

The operational record should identify which specimens were cleaned, how they were processed, and whether they contacted a wet tool. Absolute cleanliness and a particular cleanroom classification are not assumed. The relevant target is enough reproducibility to explain and improve the intended manipulation.
