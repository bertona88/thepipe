# Vacuum and printed fluidic paths

**Promising candidate:** external pumps and valves connected to a printed distal aperture, with a channel through some or all of the arm.

## Ideal holding scale

For a sealed effective area A, the ideal pressure contribution is F ≈ Δp A. As an illustrative calculation, a 50 µm diameter circular aperture at a 20 kPa pressure difference has an area of about 1.96 × 10⁻⁹ m² and an ideal force of about 39 µN.

A solid 500 µm cube at an assumed density of 1,200 kg/m³ weighs about 1.47 µN on Earth. The comparison explains why vacuum is attractive relative to weight. It does not establish a 39 µN payload: seal quality, leakage, pressure at the aperture, lateral loads and moments govern actual retention. The cube is only a calculation specimen, not a selected part or resin.

## Seal and flow together

An annular ridge, a locally compliant lip or several small suction zones are candidate geometries. Texture intended to reduce dry adhesion can create a leak under a vacuum lip. A smooth sealing ring can itself stick after pressure equalises. The contact needs a deliberate balance between sealing, holding and separation.

Narrow channels have strong flow resistance. For a rigid circular channel carrying a Newtonian incompressible liquid in laminar flow, R_h = 8μL/(πr⁴). That formula is useful for liquid-channel intuition. A vacuum gas system also has compressibility, line volume and possibly small-scale flow effects, so its transient response needs the appropriate model or measurement.

## Integration through a flexure

A printed lumen can remove external tubing near the tip. It must remain open through bending, withstand its pressure difference and be cleared of uncured material. Pressure-induced deformation can change the tool pose. A flexible external tube across a joint is a legitimate alternative when it is easier to fabricate and qualify.

## Release

Venting removes the pressure contribution only after the distal volume equilibrates. Residual dry, capillary or electrostatic adhesion may remain. A receiving tool or fixture should support the part while the donor vents and breaks contact. A bounded gas pulse is an exploratory release method, with part displacement and contamination disturbance as key limits.

Vacuum and liquid tools can share some routing concepts but need not share one lumen. A wet tool changes the contamination and adhesion history of every contacted surface. The [capillary opportunity document](../../04-2pp-opportunities/wet-tools-and-capillary-transfer.md) treats that as a separate operating mode.
