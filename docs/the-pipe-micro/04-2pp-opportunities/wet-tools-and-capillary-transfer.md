# Wet tools and capillary transfer

**Status: exploration raised in the discussion.** A printed channel and designed wetting geometry can make a tiny liquid bridge a useful attachment mechanism.

## Candidate operation

A tip approaches a retained component. A metered liquid volume or pre-existing controlled meniscus establishes contact. The tool carries the component to a destination whose geometry supports a different liquid/contact configuration. The receiver retains the object while the donor's meniscus is reduced, displaced or transferred.

A destination wick is one candidate. A more wettable or differently shaped receiver might draw liquid away from the donor. It does not automatically take the object with it; the full contact geometry, force balance and bridge breakup determine the outcome.

## What 2PP contributes

The tip can combine a supply opening, a contact rim, a reservoir feature and textured regions controlling where liquid sits. The destination can include a wicking path or a local seat. Different geometries can be fabricated from the same resin before any surface treatment is introduced.

A published textured capillary gripper demonstrated release through a change in contact conformity. This supports the idea that surface geometry can participate in release; it is not a demonstration of the specific liquid-transfer sequence proposed here. See [the source note](../06-reference/sources-and-glossary.md).

## Potential uses

Capillary pickup may help with parts that are awkward to clamp. A liquid bridge could offer compliant support or assist alignment in a carefully designed seat. A wet tool could also deliver a controlled quantity of fluid for an assembly operation. These are separate use cases and need not share one recipe.

## Physical questions

How much liquid remains on the component? Does evaporation shift the holding force? Does a bridge rotate the object during separation? Can a channel be cleared and refilled reproducibly? Does the chosen liquid swell or alter the resin? How is residue prevented from changing later dry operations?

The chamber makes environmental control plausible, but intentional wetting should remain an explicitly identified tool mode. Ordinary RH control and an intentional meniscus are not interchangeable actuators.
